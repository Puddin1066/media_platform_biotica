Maybe the pill isn’t the treatment. Maybe it’s the user interface: a physical push notification telling your nervous system, care has begun. In a small 2016 randomized trial, 97 people with chronic low-back pain were told outright: these are placebo pills. Three weeks later, the placebo-plus-usual-care group reported larger drops in pain and disability than usual care alone. So no, this isn’t Santa medicine. Explicit belief may not be the only employee in the company. Ritual. Expectation. Learned association. Attention. A daily cue. A check-in. Repeat. The pill may be pharmacologically inert. The customer experience is not. That’s why Ted Kaptchuk’s Harvard-wide program at Beth Israel Deaconess studies the therapeutic encounter—not just fake pills. Controlled pain experiments have linked analgesic expectation to measurable opioid-system activity. A phone can shape expectations; whether it replaces the physical ritual is the experiment. So if you’re selling a placebo protocol with a gummy and an app, ask the rude question: what is the shipment doing that the phone cannot?
